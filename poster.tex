% Block 1: the hotspot and the design goal. 43.5 % is report_baseline.md.
\begin{block}{1 \ The bottleneck}
  InSAR time-series analysis is a standard tool for measuring ground
  deformation from volcanoes, earthquakes and land subsidence. With
  Sentinel-1 acquiring continuously and NISAR~[1] now in orbit, archives
  grow faster than the throughput of the software that consumes them.

  \bignumber{43.5\,\%}{of a complete MintPy~[2] run (18-step
    \texttt{smallbaselineApp}) is spent in \emph{one} step: the least-squares
    inversion of the interferogram network, solved pixel by pixel on the CPU}

  \keypoint{Goal: remove that bottleneck on a single consumer GPU, with no
    HPC cluster, no CUDA programming on the user side, and the default CPU
    behaviour left untouched.}
\end{block}

% Block 2: the shape of the problem. Pixel counts: S2 (3.4 M) and
% compute_scale.md (NISAR frame). D^3 growth: compute_scale.md.
\begin{block}{2 \ The problem: millions of small least-squares systems}
  Each pixel $k$ poses the same weighted least-squares problem
  \[
    \hat{x}_k = \arg\min_{x}\,\bigl\lVert W_k^{1/2}\,(G\,x - d_k)\bigr\rVert_2^2 ,
  \]
  with $K$ interferograms (network edges) and $P = D - 1$ unknowns for $D$
  acquisition dates (vertices): the design matrix $G$ is $K \times P$ and
  shared by every pixel, while the phases $d_k$ and the coherence-based
  weights $W_k$ change from pixel to pixel. A scene has $n = 10^{5}$ to
  $10^{8}$ pixels: 3.4~million on the Gal\'apagos scene, about
  $3 \times 10^{8}$ per NISAR frame.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{tall_skinny_matrix.pdf}
  \figcaption{One pixel's system drawn to scale (Fernandina: $K=288$, $P=97$).
    The 191 redundant rows are what make the network well-conditioned (block~5).}

  \raggedright
  Per-pixel cost is $O(KP^2 + P^3/3)$. As the archive lengthens,
  $K \propto D$ and $P^2 \propto D^2$, so the total work grows like $D^3$:
  one to three years of data is about $29\times$ the work.
\end{block}

% Block 3: the failed first attempt. 1.43x: report_torch.md; 82 % and the
% launch count: report_profile.md / S1 table.
\begin{block}{3 \ First attempt: batched QR on the GPU, only 1.43$\times$}
  A direct port of the per-pixel \texttt{lstsq} to a batched QR solver on the
  GPU reached only \alert{1.43$\times$} over the CPU path (Fernandina).
  Profiling put 82\,\% of the time inside the QR itself: one chunk of about
  40{,}000 pixels issued \alert{3{,}841{,}835 kernel launches}.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{qr_column_sweep.pdf}
  \figcaption{QR sweeps the $P$ columns one at a time. Batching puts the
    matrices side by side but leaves $P$ dependent steps inside each one, and
    a GPU is at its worst on many tiny, ordered operations.}

  \keypoint{The bottleneck was the shape of the problem, not the hardware.}
\end{block}

\end{column}

% Block 4: the method. The 16.5x / 4.49x pair is S1 and MUST keep its three
% qualifiers (same GPU, Fernandina, solver core). Launch counts: S1 table.
\begin{block}{4 \ Method: normal equations and one batched Cholesky}
  Reformulate each system around its normal equations,
  \[
    N_k\,x_k = r_k, \qquad N_k = G^{\!\top} W_k\, G, \quad r_k = G^{\!\top} W_k\, d_k ,
  \]
  where $N_k$ is a small $P \times P$ symmetric positive-definite block.
  One batched matrix product builds every $N_k$ of a chunk and one batched
  Cholesky call factorizes them all, in single precision with PyTorch~[5]:

  \begin{beamercolorbox}[wd=\linewidth,sep=0.7ex,leftskip=0.8em]{code box}
    \ttfamily\footnotesize
    G\_T = G\_batch.transpose(-1, -2)\\
    N = G\_T @ G\_batch\hfill\textcolor{tastiMuted}{\# Gram, batched}\\
    r = G\_T @ y\_batch.unsqueeze(-1)\hfill\textcolor{tastiMuted}{\# RHS, batched}\\
    L, info = torch.linalg.cholesky\_ex(N)\hfill\textcolor{tastiMuted}{\# rank via info}\\
    X = torch.cholesky\_solve(r, L)
  \end{beamercolorbox}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{fig1_batched_cholesky.pdf}
  \figcaption{Why the normal-equation path batches and the QR path does not.
    Kernel launches per chunk measured on Fernandina ($K=288$, $P=97$).}

  \raggedright
  \textbf{Same GPU, same scene (Fernandina), solver core only:} QR 228~s
  $\rightarrow$ Cholesky 13.8~s, \alert{16.5$\times$}; the whole step including
  I/O, 4.49$\times$. Gram matrix plus Cholesky is an established strategy for
  parallel and GPU settings, at the documented cost of squaring the condition
  number~[3,\,4]; here only the solution is computed, never a QR factor.

  \textbf{Implementation, deliberately minimally invasive:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item opt-in by one key, \texttt{mintpy.networkInversion.solver = torch};
      separate \texttt{mintpy.gpu} subpackage; CPU path byte-for-byte unchanged
    \item chunks sized to free GPU memory; $G$ uploaded once and reused
    \item missing observations enter as zero weights, which is exactly row
      deletion in $G^{\!\top} W_k G = \sum_i w_i\, g_i g_i^{\!\top}$: one batch shape
    \item rank-deficient pixels flagged by \texttt{cholesky\_ex} and zero-filled,
      so no NaN leaks downstream
    \item the same core serves the DEM-error correction by swapping $G$
  \end{itemize}
\end{block}

% Block 5: conditioning, measured. Table: report_condition_number.md @ d74df50.
% 16 um: S2 note. Keep the CholeskyQR2 sentence within "same domain" (note 9).
\begin{block}{5 \ Why the textbook warning does not bite here}
  Normal equations square the condition number, $\kappa(N_k) = \kappa(G_w)^2$,
  and in single precision ($u \approx 6 \times 10^{-8}$) Cholesky breaks down
  near $\kappa \approx u^{-1/2} \approx 4 \times 10^{3}$. The question is
  therefore the actual $\kappa$ of SBAS networks, measured here by float64 SVD
  on the five benchmark scenes:

  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
    \toprule
    Scene & $K \times D$ & $\kappa_2(G)$ & weighted $\kappa$, max & headroom \\
    \midrule
    Fernandina        & $288 \times 98$   & 11.0 & 323 & $12.7\times$ \\
    Gal\'apagos       & $475 \times 98$   & 12.7 & 242 & $16.9\times$ \\
    Kuju              & $167 \times 24$   & 28.6 & 81  & $50\times$   \\
    San Francisco Bay & $1297 \times 333$ & 55.1 & 721 & $5.7\times$  \\
    San Francisco     & $505 \times 114$  & 51.1 & (run unweighted) & $80\times$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \figcaption{2{,}100 sampled pixels per scene; headroom = $4.1 \times 10^{3}$
    over the worst sampled $\kappa$ of the system actually solved. A redundant
    SBAS network supports every direction, which keeps $\kappa_2(G)$ at $O(10)$.}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{error_budget.pdf}
  \figcaption{Largest absolute RMS difference from the CPU reference: about
    16~$\mu$m, far below the millimetre-scale signal.}

  \raggedright
  The observed differences sit two to four orders of magnitude below the
  pessimistic $\kappa^2 u$ bound of the worst pixel. The thin tail of pixels
  that would break down is the same low-coherence population that the
  standard temporal-coherence mask removes, and a QR-based scheme such as
  CholeskyQR2 shares the same applicability domain~[3].
\end{block}

\begin{block}{6 \ Results: speed}
  Every speedup is measured against the CPU reference path on one consumer
  workstation GPU (NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5080, 16~GB).

  \bignumber{6{,}189 s $\rightarrow$ 170 s}{Gal\'apagos scene, 3.4~million
    pixels $\times$ 475 retained interferograms: \textbf{36.4$\times$}
    step-level, 44.4$\times$ kernel-level, within 7.6~GB of GPU memory}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{fig2_speedup.pdf}
  \figcaption{Step-level wall time of the network inversion, extracted from
    complete 18-step runs on five public scenes (four processors, two radar
    wavelengths, all on Zenodo). No size dependence is claimed: the ranking
    does not follow pixel count.}

  \raggedright
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Same core on the DEM-error correction: 6.2$\times$ on the large
      scene; with about $1/1000$ of the per-pixel work that step is
      overhead-bound, not compute-bound.
    \item The complete 18-step workflow runs end-to-end with the GPU solvers.
  \end{itemize}
\end{block}

% Block 7: agreement gate. S3 numerical section; the caveat is the Kuju /
% SanFranSF radar-coordinate note.
\begin{block}{7 \ Results: numerical agreement}
  Agreement with the CPU reference is enforced by a gate on the normalized RMS
  difference, $\mathrm{rms}/\max|\mathrm{cpu}| < 10^{-5}$, on the final products
  (velocity, geocoded time series).
  \begin{itemize}
    \item All five scenes clear the gate on the final quality-masked products;
      the differences of order $10^{-5}$ are accumulated single-precision
      rounding, nothing more.
    \item On two scenes (Kuju, San Francisco ARIA) radar-coordinate
      intermediates differ in near-rank-deficient pixels (minimum-norm
      \texttt{lstsq} versus zero fill); the temporal-coherence mask discards
      those pixels before any product is used.
  \end{itemize}
\end{block}

% Block 8: significance. NISAR numbers are EXTRAPOLATED (compute_scale.md);
% FORMOSAT-9 stays regional context with no resolution figure (note 5);
% PR status checked 2026-10-07 -- re-check before printing.
\begin{block}{8 \ Significance and outlook}
  \keypoint{Network inversions that took hours now finish in minutes on
    hardware a single research group can own.}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Where it matters:} rapid-response monitoring of volcanoes
      and earthquakes, and routine national-scale subsidence surveillance as
      archives grow, with regional SAR capacity still expanding, including
      Taiwan's forthcoming FORMOSAT-9 constellation~[6].
    \item \textbf{NISAR scale, extrapolated from the measured throughput:}
      one frame-year is about $3 \times 10^{8}$ pixels with $D \approx 30$ and
      $K \approx 135$, a $29 \times 29$ Cholesky $3 \times 10^{8}$ times:
      roughly 4~h on the CPU against 7 to 20~min on the GPU, and the cost grows
      like $D^3$ with archive length.
    \item \textbf{Upstream:} submitted to MintPy as pull request \#1490~[7],
      under review; rebased on the current \texttt{main} (August 2026) with
      the GPU unit tests passing.
  \end{itemize}
