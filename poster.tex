% Block 2: why the inversion exists at all, for readers new to InSAR. No
% benchmark numbers. The network figure maps each edge to one equation
% (shared by every pixel: G) and to a coherence weight (per pixel: W_k). The
% "Why not just solve them?" paragraph is the crux for newcomers: around a
% triangle of dates the interferograms should add up, but noisy ones never
% do (loop closure, without naming it), so an exact solution does not exist.
% The misfit is per-interferogram noise (multilook decorrelation, unwrapping
% errors); atmosphere and real motion belong to a date and cancel around the
% loop, so never blame the atmosphere here (docs/qa_prep.md Q1c). The bowl
% figure then shows the same triangle as a minimization and how a weight moves
% the minimum, for one representative pixel only: two pixels in one figure
% read as if they were solved together. Bowl numbers are illustrative; its
% weights are the bars of the network figure. Weight = inverse phase variance from coherence is MintPy's
% weightFunc = var. Equations are drawn in displacement form; MintPy solves
% the equivalent interval-velocity form by default (docs/qa_prep.md Q1b). The
% last bullet (per-pixel weights forbid a shared factorization) is the bridge
% to block 3.
\begin{posterblock}{2}{Why a least-squares inversion?}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item An \emph{interferogram}, the phase difference of two radar images
      of the same ground, measures how far it moved toward the satellite
      between the two dates, to millimetres.
    \item Distant pairs lose coherence, so many short pairs form a
      \emph{network}: dates as vertices, interferograms as edges.
  \end{itemize}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{observation_network_example.pdf}
  \figcaption{$x_i$: displacement at date $t_i$, with $x_0 = 0$ as the
    reference.}

  \raggedright
  \textbf{Why not just solve them?}
  Moving from $t_0$ to $t_1$ and then on to $t_2$ is the same as moving from
  $t_0$ to $t_2$, so the three interferograms should satisfy
  \[
    d_{01} + d_{12} = d_{02} .
  \]
  Real interferograms each carry their own noise and never add up exactly.
  No displacement history fits them all, so the best compromise has to be
  found by optimization.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{least_squares_bowl.pdf}
  \figcaption{One pixel, illustrative numbers; the weights are the bars above.}

  \raggedright
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Weights come from coherence (inverse phase variance, MintPy's
      default) and differ per pixel, so every pixel repeats this alone:
      millions of independent small problems.
  \end{itemize}
\end{posterblock}

% Block 3: the shape of the problem. Pixel counts: S2 (3.4 M) and
% compute_scale.md (NISAR frame). The per-pixel cost O(KP^2 + P^3/3) and the
% D^3 growth (1 -> 3 years ~ 29x) moved to docs/qa_prep.md Q8a on 2026-10-26.
\begin{posterblock}{3}{The problem: millions of small least-squares systems}
  Each pixel $k$ finds the bottom of its own bowl (block~2),
  \[
    \hat{x}_k = \arg\min_{x}\,\bigl\lVert W_k^{1/2}\,(G\,x - d_k)\bigr\rVert_2^2 ,
  \]
  with $K$ interferograms (edges) and $P = D - 1$ unknowns for $D$ dates:
  $G$ ($K \times P$) is shared by every pixel, the phases $d_k$ and weights
  $W_k$ are not. A scene has $n = 10^{5}$ to $10^{8}$ pixels: 3.4~million
  on the Gal\'apagos scene, about $3 \times 10^{8}$ per NISAR frame.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{tall_skinny_matrix.pdf}
  \figcaption{One pixel's system, to scale (Fernandina: $K=288$, $P=97$).}
\end{posterblock}

% Block 4: the method, framed by the failed direct port. Both figures draw
% one chunk on the same grid, pixels by ordered steps: the QR port fills it
% one cell at a time, the normal-equation path covers each step for every
% pixel at once. Checked against the code: PyTorch 2.11 linalg.lstsq uses
% cuBLAS gelsBatched only up to 256 rows, so at K = 288 it loops over the
% pixels (geqrf + ormqr per pixel); PR #1490 (_solve_cholesky @ 086d162)
% builds every N_k with one batched GEMM, then cholesky_ex + cholesky_solve.
% Launch counts are S1 (report_solver_comparison.md section 4). 1.43x is
% Phase 1 (report_torch.md), 16.5x is S1; both are MintPy-reported step
% times with interpreter start-up excluded, NOT solver-only numbers. Never
% quote the S1 step wall (4.49x): ~47 s of start-up sits on both sides. The
% GEMM's share of the arithmetic stays qualitative (numbers: docs/qa_prep.md).
% Digest only: no code box and no implementation tips (chunking, rank flags,
% opt-in key); those live in docs/qa_prep.md and on GitHub (block 7's QR codes).
\begin{posterblock}{4}{Method: reformulate for the GPU, don't just port}
  \keypoint{Porting the CPU solver to a GPU is not enough: the problem has to
    be recast into a shape the GPU is good at.}

  \textbf{A direct port falls short.}
  Handing the pixels to PyTorch's QR least-squares solver on the GPU reached
  only \alert{1.43$\times$} over the CPU path (Fernandina).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{qr_launch_grid.pdf}
  \figcaption{One chunk of 19{,}403 pixels ($K = 288$, $P = 97$). At this
    matrix height PyTorch's QR solver works through the pixels one at a time.}

  \raggedright
  \textbf{Recast around the normal equations,}
  \[
    N_k\,x_k = r_k, \qquad N_k = G^{\!\top} W_k\, G, \quad r_k = G^{\!\top} W_k\, d_k ,
  \]
  the same solution in exact arithmetic (its accuracy cost:
  block~6), in single precision with PyTorch~[5] and no custom GPU code.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{normal_equations_batched.pdf}
  \figcaption{The same chunk, with $G_w = W_k^{1/2} G$. Missing interferograms
    enter as zero weights, so every pixel keeps the same shape and the chunk
    stays one batch.}

  \raggedright
  \textbf{Same GPU, same scene (Fernandina):} QR 228~s $\rightarrow$ Cholesky
  13.8~s, \alert{16.5$\times$}. Both ratios in this block are step times
  reported by MintPy, with interpreter start-up excluded.
  Gram matrix plus Cholesky is an established strategy for
  parallel and GPU settings, at the documented cost of squaring the condition
  number~[3,\,4]; here only the solution is computed, never a QR factor.
\end{posterblock}

% Block 6: accuracy. Logic for a non-specialist: what kappa means (a matrix
% flattens the unit circle into an ellipse, solving stretches it back and the
% rounding blur with it; too flat and the short axis is lost) -> the normal
% equations flatten twice, so the textbook bad choice is a mathematical fact
% -> the cost matters only if it reaches what InSAR resolves -> measured.
% No claim that a denser network lowers kappa: that is a rule of thumb, and
% the measured tail comes from the weights. Table: report_condition_number.md
% @ d74df50. Error: S2 Galapagos diff JSON (per-pixel RMS vs CPU, median
% 0.065 um, max 16.4 um). Gate: S3 numerical section (5/5 on final
% products). This block absorbed the former "numerical agreement" block; the
% radar-coordinate caveat and CholeskyQR2 are left to docs/qa_prep.md.
% The CPU reference is scipy.linalg.lstsq (default driver gelsd, SVD-based).
\begin{posterblock}{6}{The accuracy cost: micrometres, against millimetre signals}
  A matrix flattens some directions more than others; its
  \emph{condition number} $\kappa$ is how flat, the longest axis over the
  shortest.
  Solving stretches them back, rounding error included, up to $\kappa$ times.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{condition_number_flatten_restore.pdf}
  \figcaption{The rounding blur is the same size in every direction:
    $u \approx 6 \times 10^{-8}$ of the longest axis in single precision.
    A short axis thinner than that
    ($\kappa \gtrsim 1/u \approx 1.7 \times 10^{7}$) cannot be restored.}

  \raggedright
  The normal equations flatten twice: $N_k = G_w^{\top} G_w$ with
  $G_w = W_k^{1/2} G$, so $\kappa(N_k) = \kappa(G_w)^2$, and single-precision
  Cholesky fails outright once $\kappa(G_w)$ nears
  $u^{-1/2} \approx 4.1 \times 10^{3}$.
  That is why textbooks solve least squares with QR or SVD, as the CPU path
  does.
  The cost is worth paying only if real networks stay clear of that point and
  the extra error stays below what InSAR resolves, so we measured both.

  \par\vskip0.4em
  \centering
  {\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}\arrayrulecolor{tastiNavy}
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule[0.8mm]
    Scene & $\kappa$, unweighted & $\kappa$, worst weighted pixel & margin \\
    \midrule[0.4mm]
    Fernandina        & 11.0 & 323 & \margin{12.7} \\
    Gal\'apagos       & 12.7 & 242 & \margin{16.9} \\
    Kuju              & 28.6 & 81  & \margin{50}   \\
    San Francisco Bay & 55.1 & 721 & \margin{5.7}  \\
    San Francisco     & 51.1 & (run unweighted) & \margin{80} \\
    \bottomrule[0.8mm]
  \end{tabular}\par}
  \figcaption{The five scenes of the speed chart (block~5).
    Float64 SVD on 2{,}100 sampled pixels per scene. Margin is the
    distance from the worst $\kappa$ of the system actually solved to the
    breakdown point, $4.1 \times 10^{3}$.}

  \raggedright
  Against the CPU reference, the per-pixel RMS difference of the time series
  is 0.06~$\mu$m for the median pixel and 16~$\mu$m for the worst one
  (Gal\'apagos, 3.4~million pixels).
  All five scenes pass an agreement gate, RMS difference below $10^{-5}$ of
  the largest CPU value, on the final products (velocity and geocoded time
  series).
  The few pixels that would break down are low-coherence ones that the
  standard temporal-coherence mask discards anyway.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{error_budget.pdf}

  \keypoint{Against the CPU's SVD-based solver, squaring $\kappa$ does leave a
    trace, but at micrometres, buried under InSAR's other error sources.}
\end{posterblock}

\begin{posterblock}{7}{Why it matters: SAR data is multiplying}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item NISAR~[1] maps nearly all of Earth's land and ice every 12~days.
    \item Commercial constellations of small SAR satellites add dozens more.
    \item Taiwan's own SAR satellites, FORMOSAT-9~[6], are planned.
  \end{itemize}
  More acquisitions mean longer archives, whose inversion cost grows roughly
  with the cube of their length, and every new acquisition means inverting the
  whole network again.
  Turning that step from hours on a CPU into minutes on one consumer GPU makes
  routine wide-area monitoring and archive reprocessing practical without an
  HPC cluster.

  \keypoint{As SAR archives multiply, a consumer GPU keeps their time-series
    analysis within reach of any research group.}
